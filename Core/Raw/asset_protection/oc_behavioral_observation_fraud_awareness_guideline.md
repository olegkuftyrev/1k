# ORDINA

## Behavioral Observation & Fraud Awareness Guideline

### Summary

Changes in behavior, work habits, and operational performance may indicate operational issues, policy violations, or potential fraud.

Behavioral observations should never be used alone to determine misconduct. Observations must always be supported by facts, documentation, operational reports, audits, or direct evidence.

The purpose of this guideline is to increase awareness, not to make accusations.

---

# Purpose

Help leaders recognize unusual behaviors and operational changes that may require additional review.

This guideline applies to all managers and leaders.

---

# Establishing Normal Behavior

Every associate has different communication styles, personalities, and work habits.

Managers should understand:

- Normal work behavior.
- Communication style.
- Usual performance.
- Typical interaction patterns.
- Standard operating habits.

Behavior changes are often more important than behavior itself.

---

# Behavioral Changes

The following changes may warrant additional observation:

- Increased nervousness.
- Sudden defensiveness.
- Avoiding leadership interaction.
- Unusual isolation.
- Changes in attitude.
- Increased complaints or frustration.
- Avoiding accountability conversations.
- Delayed responses to routine questions.

These behaviors alone do not indicate misconduct.

---

# Operational Warning Signs

Managers should pay attention to:

- Frequent cash shortages.
- Excessive refunds.
- Unusual discounts.
- Increased voids.
- Register irregularities.
- Inventory variances.
- Repeated policy violations.
- Unusual schedule requests.
- Unexplained performance changes.

Operational data should always support concerns.

---

# Register Area Concerns

Leaders should observe:

- Clutter around registers.
- Unauthorized notes or tracking sheets.
- Manager cards or keys left unattended.
- Shared login credentials.
- Unsecured access devices.
- Unauthorized use of manager functions.

Security procedures should always be followed.

---

# Body Language Considerations

Body language may provide additional context but should never be considered proof of wrongdoing.

Examples include:

- Closed body posture.
- Unusual stiffness.
- Reduced eye contact.
- Delayed answers.
- Increased anxiety.
- Changes from normal behavior.

Stress, personal issues, fatigue, or workplace pressure may also cause these behaviors.

---

# Investigation Standards

Managers should:

- Focus on facts.
- Review reports and documentation.
- Conduct audits.
- Verify information.
- Maintain confidentiality.
- Avoid assumptions.

Behavioral observations should support investigations, not replace evidence.

---

# Management Responsibilities

Leaders are expected to:

- Observe fairly.
- Remain objective.
- Protect associates.
- Maintain confidentiality.
- Follow company procedures.
- Escalate concerns appropriately.

False accusations can cause significant harm and should be avoided.

---

# Guiding Principle

Observe behavior.

Verify facts.

Act on evidence.

Protect both the business and the associate through objective decision-making.
